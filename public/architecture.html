How it works Architecture GitHub

Architecture

Three layers. A weekly build turns FDA label files into a search index and lookup tables. Two stateless Python functions answer requests. A static page talks to them.

Where the data comes from

Every clinical statement the tool shows is a sentence from an FDA label. The labels come from openFDA, the FDA's open data program. It publishes every Structured Product Label (SPL) as JSON, in the public domain, through both a REST API and bulk download files, and refreshes them weekly. Each record carries the label's named sections (boxed warning, indications, contraindications, warnings and precautions, drug interactions, dosage, adverse reactions, and dozens more) plus harmonized openfda metadata: brand, generic, and substance names, route, pharmacologic class, application number, and the SPL set id that DailyMed uses.

The whole export is downloaded, then filtered down to one current label per active-ingredient set. The scope rules come from Part 1 of this project.

Three more sources play smaller, exact roles.

The weekly build

Every Monday, and on every change to the code, a GitHub Actions job rebuilds everything from the bulk files and deploys. openFDA updates weekly; this runs the day after.

  1. 1DownloadThe openFDA drug-label bulk files. 262,883 label records.
  2. 2CollectApply the funnel above. Keep the newest label per ingredient set. 2,481 labels remain.
  3. 3Map ingredientsRoll every substance up to its base ingredient through RxNorm.
  4. 4TagRun the 17 vocabulary rules on every label. Keep the sentence behind each tag.
  5. 5IndexBuild the BM25 index, the name dictionary, and the checker's per-label tables.
  6. 6Test and deploy353 Python tests and 109 front-end tests must pass. Then the built files go to Vercel.

The vocabulary

Prescription Drug Label Attributes (PDLA) is a controlled vocabulary of 17 terms, built in Part II of this project. It tags the properties pharmacists, clinicians, and patients filter on: safety signals, route, patient group, dosing, DEA control, product form, and interaction risk. Each term is assigned by one fixed rule over named openFDA fields, so the same label always gets the same terms. Two rules apply to every term: if a field a rule needs is missing, the term is not assigned; and a missing term means the label did not show the condition, not that the opposite is true.

Hierarchy

A shallow hierarchy, not a flat list: ten top-level terms, and seven narrower terms under three of them. Every edge is one relationship, is a type of: every label that meets the narrower term's definition also meets the broader term's. Top-level terms are not linked to each other because each describes a different property of a label, and none implies another.

The three parents work in two ways. T07 has its own rule (any DEA schedule II to V), so T10 marks a subset it already covers; the link keeps them consistent. T11 and T13 have no rule of their own. They exist only through their children: a label gets T11 only when it gets T06 or T12, and T13 only when it gets one of T14 to T17.

Expansion at indexing time

A narrower term brings its broader term, and it happens when labels are tagged, not when a user searches. When the tagger assigns T06, it also writes T11 to that label's index entry; when it assigns any of T14 to T17, it writes T13. A search for T13 is then a single lookup of a stored tag, and returns the same labels that T13 OR T14 OR T15 OR T16 OR T17 would under query expansion. It does not work in reverse: a search for T14 does not return labels with only a QT warning. Indexing-time expansion was chosen because it matches the term definitions, keeps every stored tag complete for facet counts and AND/OR combinations, and costs only a re-tag inside the weekly rebuild.

Terms are not mutually exclusive. A label gets every term whose rule it meets; the OxyContin label carries at least T01, T02, T05, T07, T08, T09, T10, T13, and T15. The only constraints are the hierarchy's (no T10 without T07, no T11 without T06 or T12, no T13 without one of T14 to T17) and the fact that a product sits in one DEA schedule.

Tagging

Each rule reads its fields in order and applies a pattern. The first sentence that matches becomes the term's evidence. A term with no evidence is never assigned. For the four interaction-risk terms:

TermWhat the rule requires
T14 Serotonin Syndrome RiskA sentence in a warning, precaution, interaction, or contraindication field that names serotonin syndrome or serotonin toxicity as a risk. A mention only in mechanism of action does not count.
T15 CNS Depression RiskA warning of added CNS depression, sedation, or respiratory depression with other CNS depressants or alcohol. Drowsiness listed alone as an adverse reaction does not count.
T16 QT Prolongation RiskA warning of QT prolongation or torsades de pointes. A study result showing no effect does not count.
T17 Contraindicated CombinationA sentence in contraindications that names another drug, or a class from the FDA tables, that must not be used with this drug. "Avoid" in warnings is weaker and does not count. The drug's own name and hypersensitivity clauses are ignored.

Ranking

Search runs in two stages, and they are different kinds of relevance. A vocabulary filter is a set: a label is in or out, with no degree. BM25 is a score: labels are ordered, not selected. The filter runs first and the score ranks what remains.

  1. 1FilterSelected terms narrow the set: every term (AND, the intersection) or any term (OR, the union).
  2. 2RankThe remaining labels are scored with BM25 and sorted, best first.
  3. 3Snippet80 characters either side of the first query word in the label's text.

A document is one label: its indexed text fields joined with its openFDA brand, generic, and substance names. Tokens are lowercased runs of letters and digits. There is no stemming and no stop-word list, so "spasm" and "spasms" are different terms; this keeps ranking simple and predictable at the cost of some recall. The average document is 3,872 tokens.

score(D, Q) = Σt ∈ Q  IDF(t) · tf(t, D) · (k₁ + 1) / ( tf(t, D) + k₁ · (1 − b + b · |D| / avgdl) )

IDF(t) = ln( (N − n(t) + 0.5) / (n(t) + 0.5) + 1 )

k₁ = 1.2    b = 0.75    N = 2,481    avgdl = 3,872
SymbolMeaning
tf(t, D)How many times term t occurs in label D.
n(t)How many labels contain t. Rare terms score higher.
|D| / avgdlThis label's length against the average. Long labels are penalized so they don't win on bulk.
k₁How quickly repeated occurrences stop adding score.
bHow much length matters, from 0 (not at all) to 1 (fully).

Name resolution

A typed entry is parsed into a brand, a generic, a strength per component, a frequency or named-day schedule, and any as-needed maximum; a stated total after "=" is kept as written and never read as a dose. The name is then matched in stages. The first stage that succeeds wins.

  1. 0Written genericThe generic in parentheses is rolled up to base ingredients (divalproex sodium → valproate) and matched to the label with exactly those ingredients, preferring the typed brand's label. An OTC ingredient with no single-ingredient label (aspirin, calcium carbonate) is kept by ingredient so the class rules still apply.
  2. 1Exact matchAgainst the local dictionary of every brand, generic, and ingredient name in the collection.
  3. 2Close matchSequence similarity against the dictionary, whole name and leading words. Accepted at 0.85 or better. "trazdone" matches "trazodone".
  4. 3RxNorm approximate matchRxNav's approximateTerm, then the concept's ingredients, then the collection label for that ingredient set. An exact RxNorm name beats a weaker local guess.
  5. 4Ask, don't guessIf two products fit equally, the entry is marked "needs confirmation" and the choices are shown. Salt forms of one molecule are settled automatically.

The checker

One request parses, resolves, adds up each ingredient, then runs four kinds of check. The label-driven alerts are folded into the class rule that covers them, as evidence.

Group Two or more medicines share an effect class: CNS depression, serotonin, QT, bleeding, potassium, kidney injury, blood pressure, heart rate, glucose, anticholinergic load, seizure threshold, sodium. Medicines are counted, not ingredients: Adderall's two amphetamines are one. The title states the count and the risk.
Pair A victim class meets a perpetrator class: CYP3A4, 2D6, 1A2, 2C9, 2C19, P-glycoprotein, inducers, antagonists. Hits for one victim merge into one alert ("warfarin with 2 drugs that raise the INR"). A label dose cap raises the grade when the dose is above it.
Duplication Two entries share an ingredient, or one is the other's cited active metabolite; or two different drugs of one therapeutic class (two SSRIs, two NSAIDs). Different names for one drug grade D; a regimen split over named days is not a duplicate.
Ceiling An ingredient's total across all entries, combination products included, against its labeled daily maximum.
  1. 1ParseBrand, generic, strengths, schedule, maximum.
  2. 2ResolveWritten generic, dictionary, RxNorm.
  3. 3TotalsPer ingredient, units normalized, MME.
  4. 4Rules90 class rules; label T14–T17; duplicates; ceilings.
  5. 5ReplyGrade, count, label sentence, why, what to do, sources.

The rule table is data/reference/interaction_knowledge.json. Class membership draws on the FDA table of CYP substrates, inhibitors, and inducers and on the ONC high-priority interaction list (Phansalkar 2012). At build time, each rule is matched against the Contraindications, Boxed Warning, Warnings and Precautions, and Drug Interactions sections of every label on its side, and the first sentence naming the partner drug or class is stored for quoting.

Severity grading

Every alert is graded A to E. A class rule carries its own grade, set from the clinical rulebook and the labels' own words (contraindication E, boxed warning or "avoid" D, a dose change or level to monitor C). It is raised when a label dose cap is exceeded. A label contraindication with no covering rule is graded from its section, by the list below. A label-only shared-risk tag with no covering rule is kept as a grade A note.

  1. Is it a pair alert, from the Contraindications section?E
  2. From Contraindications or the Boxed Warning, or does it say "avoid" or "not recommended" about using the drugs together?D
  3. From Warnings and Precautions, Warnings, or Precautions?C
  4. From Drug Interactions, and says to monitor or adjust the dose?B
  5. From Drug Interactions, no action stated.A

For label-driven alerts only. Read top to bottom; the first "yes" sets the grade. A duplicate is D when one drug appears under different names, in a combination product, or as a metabolite, or when the combined dose reaches its maximum; C when one product is simply entered twice.

Why the label sectionFDA's labeling rule defines each section by seriousness. Contraindications: the risk clearly outweighs any benefit. Boxed Warning: serious or life-threatening risk. (21 CFR 201.57)
Why Lexicomp's levelsClinicians already read A/B/C/D/X. Each grade here carries Lexicomp's action: avoid, consider changing therapy, monitor, or no action.
Why not copy a databaseCommercial databases disagree with each other on severity: Fleiss' κ = 0.16 across Drugs.com, Lexicomp, and Epocrates (Pinkoh et al., 2023). A grade you can trace to a sentence is easier to check.

Evaluation

The checker is measured the way retrieval systems are: a fixed collection, a fixed set of test inputs, and relevance judgments made in advance. The test set is a blind fixture of 41 patient medication lists, written the way a prescriber would write them, with an answer key of 196 expected interactions (drugs, category, severity) plus per-drug daily totals and pairs that should not be flagged. The harness sends every list to the live API, in four entry-order and separator variants, and scores each expected item against the alerts returned.

ResultCountMeaning
Hit29An alert covers the expected drugs, under an equivalent category, at the expected severity or higher.
Partial17The drugs are covered, but the category or severity differs.
Miss, no rule127The expected category (CYP inhibition, bleeding, nephrotoxicity, and 26 others) has no rule in this checker.
Miss, rule too narrow23The category is covered, but the specific pair or group is not reached.
False positive1An alert at moderate or higher on a pair the key says not to flag.

Strict recall against the key is 29 of 196, or 14.8%; counting partials, 23.5%. The gap is by design and is stated rather than hidden: the vocabulary covers four interaction-risk types plus duplication, and 127 of the 150 misses fall outside those types. Precision cannot be read from this fixture alone, because an alert the key does not mention is not thereby wrong; the one false positive is the only case the key rules out. Every list, every expected item, and what fired for it is in the validation report.

Does the grade agree with the key?

For the 46 items where an alert covered the right drugs, the tool's grade is compared with the key's severity. E maps to contraindicated, D to major, C and B to moderate, A to minor, following Pinkoh et al.'s correspondence for Lexicomp.

The main gap is 11 items the key calls major that the tool grades moderate. In those, the label states the risk only in Warnings and Precautions, with no boxed warning and no "avoid". Two causes: serotonin and QT stacks, where the labels are softer than clinical references; and every duplicate is graded C, even for an opioid, where doubling the dose is major. The rule is not tuned to fit these 41 lists.

Two more harnesses exist in the code. The tagger can be scored per term (precision, recall, F1) against a hand-labeled gold file; only a placeholder gold file is committed so far, and it is never used as ground truth. The search index can be scored with precision@k, recall@k, mean average precision, and nDCG against a queries-and-judgments file; no judgments have been recorded yet.

How it is kept honest

  • A tag with no evidence sentence is never assigned. Label sentences are quoted exactly, with section and DailyMed link. Each class rule's mechanism and action is written once and cites its rulebook row and sources.
  • The checker never says "safe", only "No warning found in the labels checked", and says "Nothing was checked" when no entry matched a drug. Two control lists must raise nothing at grade B or above.
  • Every function, in Python and TypeScript, has a three-line comment saying what it takes, does, and gives. A test fails the build if one is missing.
  • A black-box test pack drives the live site in a real browser, 41 patient lists plus 6 generalization lists, and every run is committed under test_runs/, misses included.
