How it works

You type a medication list. The tool finds each drug's FDA label, reads what the labels say about taking them together, and shows the exact sentence behind every alert.

  1. 1TypeBrand or generic. Misspellings are fine.
  2. 2ResolveEach entry is matched to one FDA label.
  3. 3CheckThe labels are read for shared risks and named combinations.
  4. 4GradeEach interaction gets a severity, A to E.
  5. 5ShowWorst first, with the label's own words.

1. Type

The box is already focused when the page opens. Type one medication and press Return, or paste a whole list. Commas, semicolons, and new lines all work as separators. If you include a dose and frequency, the tool reads them and shows a daily total.

2. Resolve

Each entry goes through two matchers in order. The first is a dictionary built from the labels themselves. The second is the National Library of Medicine's RxNorm service, which handles typos and unusual spellings.

  1. 1Local dictionaryEvery brand, generic, and ingredient name in the collection. An exact match wins. A close match (85% similar or better) also wins.
  2. 2RxNormAsked only when the dictionary finds nothing close. Returns the nearest drug concept and its ingredients.
  3. 3One labelThe newest label for that ingredient set. Salts and their parent drug count as the same ingredient.

Three outcomes are possible, and each is shown plainly in the list:

MatchedThe name the label uses, with the dose read from your entry.
Which one?The name fits more than one product. You pick. Nothing is guessed.
Not a medicationThe word is looked up in the label text instead. Your own drugs' labels come first.

3. Check

An alert is raised in three situations. All three come from label text. Nothing else is consulted.

Shared risk Two or more of your medications carry the same interaction warning in their labels. Serotonin syndrome · CNS depression · QT prolongation
Named combination One medication's label lists another of your medications, or its drug class, as contraindicated. "…is contraindicated with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors"
Same drug twice Two entries share an active ingredient, or one is the other's active metabolite. Zoloft and sertraline · venlafaxine and desvenlafaxine

4. Grade

Every interaction gets a severity, from A (minor) to E (avoid).

Each grade lines up with a level in Lexicomp, the reference hospitals license. When an interaction has several label sentences, the most severe one sets the grade. The card always says why.

5. Show

An alert names the drugs, the tier, the sentence from the label, which drug's label it came from and which section, and a link to that label on DailyMed. Nothing is paraphrased.

What it doesn't do

It reports what the FDA labels state and nothing more. "No warning found in the labels checked" means the labels didn't say it, not that a combination is safe. It is not a licensed drug interaction database and does not replace clinical judgment.

How well it does on a blind test of 41 patient medication lists is measured and published, misses included: validation report.
