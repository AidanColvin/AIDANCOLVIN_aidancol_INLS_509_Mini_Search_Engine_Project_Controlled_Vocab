# Psychotropic DDI Identification Across Drugs.com, Lexicomp, and Epocrates (Pinkoh et al., 2023)

Notes drawn only from the article text. Citation in AMA style.

## Aims
- Primary: prevalence and associated factors of psychotropic drug-drug interactions (DDIs) over a one-year observation in the psychiatric inpatient department of one Thai medical school.
- Secondary: psychotropic DDI identification utility of Drugs.com®, Lexicomp®, and Epocrates® in psychiatric hospitalisation at a quaternary hospital.

## Background
- Polypharmacy is common in psychiatric care. Patients unresponsive to standard treatment are often given multiple psychotropic classes.
- Longer life expectancy brings more medical comorbidities, which adds to polypharmacy and DDI risk.
- Prior DDI prevalence: nursing home 81.1%; community pharmacies 26.0–49.6%; hospital inpatient and outpatient departments 22.3%.
- DDIs are associated with hospitalisation or hospital visits, but few patients show clinical complications.
- Too frequent, false, and unnecessary alarms harm both provider exhaustion and patient safety. The tool should balance alarm fatigue against patient safety.
- Electronic DDI databases vary in sensitivity and comprehensiveness.
- Prior Thai studies compared Micromedex® and Drugs.com®, or Micromedex®, Drugs.com®, and Liverpool HIV interactions. They did not identify psychotropic DDIs and were not done in real clinical situations.

## Methods
- Design: retrospective cohort.
- Setting: psychiatric inpatient unit, one university hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. All admissions during 2020.
- Data: electronic health records. All prescriptions from the first day of admission to discharge or the end of the study.
- Inclusion: all patients over 18. No sample size calculation or sampling.
- Ethics: Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University. Informed consent not obtained (service records, reported anonymously).
- Clinical data: age, gender, psychiatric and medical diagnoses, each agent, dosage, and date of prescription.
- Outcomes: recorded adverse drug events, length of hospitalisation, readmission within one year after discharge.
- Included: interactions with nutritional supplements.
- Excluded: interactions with alcohol, smoking, and illicit substances (outside scope; not permitted during hospitalisation).
- None of the three databases is a standard under national or institutional policy.

### DDI definition (verbatim)
> Drugs prescribed simultaneously during hospitalisation and found to potentially develop DDI from each database would be reported as an event of DDI.

## Database severity scales

### Drugs.com®
Independent medicine information website for healthcare professionals and consumers. Its database is powered by several organisations and peer-reviewed.

| Level | Definition (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| Major | Highly clinically significant and avoid combinations; the risk of DDI outweighs the benefit |
| Moderate | Moderately clinically significant and usually avoid combinations; use only under special circumstances |
| Minor | Minimally clinically significant; minimise risk, assess risk and consider an alternative drug |
| Unknown | No DDI information available |

### Lexicomp®
DDI database for clinicians. Subscription required. Available as mobile applications, online, or desktop software.

| Level | Definition (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| A | No known interaction, neither PK nor PD DDI is demonstrated |
| B | No action needed, little to no evidence of clinical concern |
| C | Monitor therapy, clinically significant but benefit usually outweighs risk, dosage adjustment may be needed |
| D | Consider therapy modification, patient-specific assessment, aggressive monitoring, empiric dosage changes, or alternative agents |
| X | Avoid combination, clinically significant and risk outweighs benefit, and generally considered contraindicated |

### Epocrates®
DDI checking plus pill identification, medical calculator, and information resources for patients.
- Four levels: Contraindicated; Avoid/Use Alternative; Monitor/Modify Therapy; Caution Advised.
- The article gives no definitions for these levels.

## Statistics
- SPSS version 29.0 (IBM).
- Categorical variables: counts and percentages. Continuous variables: mean ± SD or median [IQR], by distribution.
- Fleiss' kappa for severity agreement across databases: 0.81–1.00 almost perfect; 0.61–0.80 substantial; 0.41–0.60 moderate; 0.21–0.40 fair; 0.00–0.20 slight; below 0.00 poor.
- Pearson or Spearman correlation for DDI count vs associated variables.
- All tests two-tailed; P ≤ 0.05 significant.

## Results: DDI counts
- 148 of 149 admissions (99.3%) had at least one psychotropic DDI.
- 956 DDI patterns and 2,825 psychotropic DDI pairs across all databases.
- DDIs identified per database (Results section): Drugs.com® 2,500; Lexicomp® 2,269; Epocrates® 2,265.
- Serious-category DDIs: Drugs.com® Major 254; Lexicomp® X 26; Epocrates® Contraindicated 17.
- Top three pairs: clonazepam with quetiapine (n = 56), risperidone (n = 36), and valproic acid and derivatives (n = 36).
- Serious DDIs were lower in incidence, with no evidence of association with reported clinical adverse consequences.

## Discussion
- The one admission with no psychotropic DDI was a patient admitted for drug dependence rehabilitation, prescribed vitamin supplements and supportive therapies.
- The results confirm a high prevalence of database-detected psychotropic DDIs in psychiatric inpatient settings.
- A median of 9 medications limits generalisability to psychiatric outpatients, who take fewer. Quaternary hospital patients often had more complex medical comorbidities.
- The top three agents causing DDIs (quetiapine, clonazepam, trazodone) were used to treat sleep disturbances.
  - Quetiapine is widely prescribed off-label for sleep because it has lower addictive qualities than benzodiazepines.
  - Clonazepam and trazodone are known for sleep disorder management; trazodone especially in older patients or patients with depression.
- Clinical adverse reactions were recorded in only 6 of 149 admissions. Most were explained by a single idiosyncratic agent.
  - The exception: a patient with schizophrenia on olanzapine and clonazepam, then given trazodone, who developed retrograde amnesia. The authors say this could be caused by DDI.
- Insomnia psychosocial interventions during hospitalisation could lower both sedative prescribing and psychotropic DDIs.
- Why the correlations:
  - More medications → more DDIs.
  - Older age and comorbidities → polypharmacy risk.
  - DDI counts were cumulative, so longer stays and readmissions show more DDIs.
  - Antiepileptics have frequent DDIs with psychotropics, mostly pharmacodynamic.
  - Many psychotropics cause orthostatic hypotension, so combinations with antihypertensives get flagged.
  - Benzodiazepines and hypnotics add sedation.
- Detected DDIs far outnumbered clinical adverse reactions. Reasons given: severity may not be high enough to cause a reaction, and providers may manage DDIs (e.g., dose adjustment). This supports alert systems that integrate clinical and informational data.
- Drugs.com® was the more sensitive tool: it detected the most DDIs, with no clinically significant events shown.
- Lexicomp® gave the most extensive medication list for this study:
  - Included drugs approved in Thailand but not in other countries (e.g., domperidone, flunarizine).
  - Gave information on interactions with herbal medicine.
  - The authors judged Lexicomp® the most suitable of the three for psychotropic DDI identification in the Thai context.
- Major-severity DDIs appeared in non-psychotropic pairs (gemfibrozil–simvastatin; omeprazole–rilpivirine) and a psychotropic/non-psychotropic pair (flunarizine–orphenadrine). No patient on these pairs reported a clinical adverse reaction.
- Dual-psychotropic DDIs were far more common but rated less severe. Some, like antipsychotics with trihexyphenidyl, are broadly prescribed in psychiatric practice.

## Limitations (as stated)
- Retrospective extraction; some information or associated factors may be missing or unrecorded.
- The hospital may have warned on, and blocked, some prescriptions with severe DDIs.
- Diagnoses were mainly schizophrenia spectrum and major depressive disorders, so drugs for other indications were less prescribed and analysed.
- The low rate of clinical adverse reactions meant their association with DDIs could not be well identified.
- Single quaternary hospital; results may not generalise to other settings or populations.

## Future directions (as stated)
- Develop updated psychotropic DDI databases for specific populations.
- Compare databases by drug mechanism to help professionals choose the most suitable one.
- Focus alerts on clinical significance, not just theoretical interactions, to prevent alarm fatigue.

## Conclusion
- Almost all psychiatric inpatients had at least one psychotropic DDI, but clinical adverse consequences were rarely reported.
- Drugs.com® detected the most interactions. Lexicomp® provided the broadest list of medications.
- Databases should be built on both theoretical and clinical conceptions to balance patient safety against provider weariness.

## Internal inconsistencies in the source text
Check the PDF of record before citing any of these numbers.
- **Database counts swapped.** Abstract: Epocrates® 2,269 and Lexicomp® 2,265. Results: Lexicomp® 2,269 and Epocrates® 2,265.
- **Total agents.** Abstract says 182; Results says 176.
- **Schizophrenia spectrum count.** Results text says n = 42 (32.8%); Table 1 says 45 (36.7%).
- **Drugs.com® scale.** Methods say "three levels" but list four (Major, Moderate, Minor, Unknown).
- **Lexicomp® scale.** Methods list five levels (A, B, C, D, X); the Discussion says Lexicomp® has 4 severity classifications.
- **Correlation labels.**
  - Antihypertensive (0.468) and antiepileptic (0.416) are "strongly correlated" in Results but "moderate" in the Discussion.
  - Underlying diseases (0.331) is "moderate" in Results but grouped under "weak" in the Discussion.
- **Hypolipidemic agents.** Listed as 17 (3.3%) of 1,357 prescriptions; 17/1,357 is 1.3%.
