How it works

You type a medication list. The tool finds each drug's FDA label, reads what the labels say about taking them together, and shows the exact sentence behind every alert.

  1. 1TypeBrand or generic. Misspellings are fine.
  2. 2ResolveEach entry is matched to one FDA label.
  3. 3CheckThe labels are read for shared risks and named combinations.
  4. 4GradeEach interaction gets a severity, A to E.
  5. 5ShowWorst first, with the label's own words.

1. Type

The box is already focused when the page opens. Type one medication and press Return, or paste a whole list. Commas, semicolons, and new lines all work as separators. A prescription line works as written: Zyprexa (olanzapine) 10 mg once daily = 10 mg/day. The tool reads the brand, the generic, every strength in a combination, named days (Mon/Wed/Fri), weekly doses, and as-needed maximums. Forgot the commas? "Adderall 30 mg Valium 20 mg" still becomes two medications.

Open any medication for its profile: what kind of drug it is and how it acts on the brain or body (with NIH StatPearls sources), what the FDA approved it to treat, how long it is meant to be used, and its usual and maximum doses, quoted from its own label.

2. Resolve

Each entry is matched in order. First comes the generic you wrote. Then a dictionary built from the labels themselves. Last, the National Library of Medicine's RxNorm service, which handles typos and unusual spellings.

  1. 1Written generic, then local dictionaryA generic in parentheses picks the label with exactly those ingredients. Otherwise every brand, generic, and ingredient name in the collection is tried: an exact match wins, and so does a close match (85% similar or better).
  2. 2RxNormAsked only when the dictionary finds nothing close. Returns the nearest drug concept and its ingredients.
  3. 3One labelThe newest label for that ingredient set. Salts and their parent drug count as the same ingredient.

Three outcomes are possible, and each is shown plainly in the list:

MatchedThe name the label uses, with the dose read from your entry.
Which one?The name fits more than one product. You pick. Nothing is guessed.
Not a medicationThe word is looked up in the label text instead. Your own drugs' labels come first.

3. Check

First every ingredient is added up across the list. Then four kinds of check run.

Class rules 90 rules built from a clinical rulebook of the interactions that actually send patients to hospital. Each rule names drug classes, not single drugs, so it also catches drugs never tested. 3 CNS depressants · statin + strong CYP3A4 inhibitor · warfarin + drugs that raise the INR
Named in a label One medication's label lists another of your medications, or its class, as contraindicated. "…is contraindicated with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors"
Same drug twice Two entries share an active ingredient, including inside a combination product or under a different name, or one is the other's active metabolite. Zoloft and sertraline · Norco and Tylenol · Risperdal and Invega
Over the maximum An ingredient's total across every entry is above its labeled daily maximum. acetaminophen 4,300 mg a day from Norco + Tylenol

4. Grade

Every interaction gets a severity, from A (minor) to E (avoid).

Each grade lines up with a level in Lexicomp, the reference hospitals license. A rule's grade can rise with the dose: simvastatin above its label cap next to diltiazem is D; at the cap it is C. When no rule covers a label's own contraindication, the label section sets the grade. The card always says why.

5. Show

An alert gives the grade, the drugs, a count and the risk ("3 CNS depressants: …"), the label sentence with its section and a DailyMed link, why it happens, what to do, and its sources. Label sentences are quoted exactly; the "why" and "what to do" are written once per rule and cite their sources.

What it doesn't do

It checks the classes and labels it knows. "No warning found in the labels checked" means neither the rules nor the labels flag the combination, not that it is safe. When no entry matches a drug, it says "Nothing was checked" instead. It is not a licensed drug interaction database and does not replace clinical judgment.

How well it does on a blind test of 41 patient medication lists, run in a real browser against this site, is measured and published, misses included: test runs.
